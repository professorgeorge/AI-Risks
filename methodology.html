01Design intent and what changed in v2

Meridian estimates how far artificial intelligence is changing the work that US degree programs prepare students for, and what kind of institutional response that implies. It covers 113 degree programs across twelve academic fields, each identified by its six-digit CIP code and, where one dominates, a mapped SOC occupational code.

The output is one of four recommendations — Pivot, Expand, Augment or Restructure — derived from a weighted composite of five dimensions: task automation potential, curriculum half-life, faculty AI readiness, regional labour market strength and employment outlook.

The tool is built for structuring a decision, not for certifying one. The scores say where to look harder and what kind of question to ask. They do not substitute for curriculum review, accreditation consultation or institution-specific labour market analysis.

What changed in version 2

Three substantive revisions, each documented in full below.

Every employment figure was re-read from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Version 1 described these as drawn from BLS; on checking, 96 of 113 did not match the Occupational Outlook Handbook, and several were wrong by enough to reverse the recommendation. All 113 are now documented: 103 read directly from the OOH entry for the program's SOC code in the current 2025–35 round, and 10 — degrees whose graduates disperse too widely for any single occupational projection to represent them — carry the BLS all-occupation projection of +3.5% instead of an asserted figure. Nothing is left as an unsupported estimate. Section 11 gives every figure, its SOC code and its source.

The wind-down rule was tightened. In v1 a program was recommended for wind-down when AI substituted for its core tasks and the composite score was high. That rule placed English, journalism, creative writing, photography, paralegal studies and medical assisting in the wind-down category — all occupations that federal projections show holding steady or growing. High task exposure was being read as falling demand. The rule now also requires that the credential map to essentially one occupation and that employment actually be projected to contract. 2 programs in 113 now meet it.

The categories were renamed. “Sunset” named a fate rather than an action, and carried a finality the underlying data cannot support. The four responses are now Pivot, Expand, Augment and Restructure. Definitions are unchanged except where noted in section 7.

The finding this produces

Once employment projections are read rather than assumed, a clear pattern appears. Of 113 programs, 7 feed an occupation that BLS projects will shrink at all, and only 2 combine that with the task substitution and the single-occupation dependence that would justify winding a program down. AI is changing what graduates do considerably faster than it is reducing how many graduates employers need. For most programs the implication is curricular, not existential — a harder problem to ignore and an easier one to act on.

This is a claim about the current evidence, not a prediction. BLS projections have historically been conservative about technological displacement, and the exposure research is only three years old. The pattern is what the public data supports today; it is exactly the kind of claim that should be re-checked when the next projection round lands.

02The data pipeline

Each program is identified by its six-digit CIP code (Classification of Instructional Programs, NCES). The CIP code is mapped to one or more SOC codes (Standard Occupational Classification) through the NCES CIP–SOC crosswalk. SOC is the unit of analysis in the Eloundou and Felten exposure research, in O*NET, and in BLS projections, so it is the hinge the whole method turns on.

Step 01CIP codeSix-digit program identifier (NCES)
Step 02SOC codeCIP–SOC crosswalk (NCES)
Step 03AI exposureEloundou et al.; Felten et al.
Step 04Task breakdownO*NET task statements and ratings
Step 05EmploymentBLS projections by SOC
Step 06CompositeWeighted formula → recommendation

Where a CIP code maps to several SOC codes — common for broad degrees such as Psychology, Business or English — exposure is weighted by the approximate share of graduates entering each occupation. That weighting is a judgement, and it is the single largest source of error in the program-level figures. 111 of 113 programs have a single dominant SOC code recorded; the remainder disperse widely enough that no one code represents them, and the application says so in the program panel rather than implying a precision it does not have.

Narrow and broad pipelines

Version 2 records, for each program, whether the credential maps to essentially one occupation (narrow: 32 programs — medical assisting, welding, court reporting, dental hygiene, most licensure-gated health credentials) or whether graduates disperse across many (broad: 81 programs — English, psychology, biology, business).

The distinction matters because occupational decline translates into graduate outcomes very differently in the two cases. When court reporting contracts, a court reporting certificate loses much of its value. When writing and editing tasks are automated, an English degree does not, because most English graduates were never going to be writers by trade. Conflating the two is what produced the wind-down recommendations that v2 removes.

03Primary data sources

All five are public, freely available, and either peer reviewed or published by a US federal agency. No proprietary vendor data is used. Publication status is labelled honestly: one of the five is a working paper, and saying so costs nothing.

Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2024)
Peer reviewed

“GPTs are GPTs: Labor market impact potential of LLMs.” Science 384(6702), 1306–1308. doi:10.1126/science.adj0998. Originally circulated as arXiv:2303.10130 (2023). Provides occupation-level exposure scores by SOC code, where α covers tasks an LLM can do alone and β covers tasks it can do with complementary tooling. Meridian uses β, which better reflects how these systems are actually deployed.

Used for: Primary AI exposure score (0–1) by SOC code, mapped to CIP through the NCES crosswalk.
Felten, Raj & Seamans
Working paper + peer-reviewed method

The 2023 language-model paper, “How will Language Modelers like ChatGPT Affect Occupations and Industries?” (arXiv:2303.01157), is a working paper and is cited as such. The underlying AI Occupational Exposure method is peer reviewed: “Occupational, industry, and geographic exposure to artificial intelligence: a novel dataset and its potential uses,” Strategic Management Journal 42(12), 2195–2217 (2021). Data at github.com/AIOE-Data/AIOE. The method links AI application areas to human abilities in O*NET, then abilities to occupations — a genuinely different route to the same question than Eloundou et al. take.

Used for: Cross-validation. Where both cover an occupation, the exposure estimate leans 60% on Eloundou β and 40% on the Felten index, reflecting the former’s wider occupational coverage.
O*NET Resource Center
US federal database

Maintained by the Employment and Training Administration, US Department of Labor. Structured task statements for every SOC occupation, each rated for importance and frequency by incumbent workers. onetcenter.org/database.html

Used for: Task breakdowns in each program panel. Statements are selected at importance ≥ 3.0 and frequency ≥ 2.5.
BLS Employment Projections and the Occupational Outlook Handbook
US federal database

Bureau of Labor Statistics. Ten-year projected employment change by SOC code, published on a rolling cycle. bls.gov/ooh; national matrix at bls.gov/emp. Note that BLS advances its projection window periodically: figures used here are drawn from the 2024–34 and 2025–35 cycles, and each program records which.

Used for: The employment outlook dimension. All 113 programs are documented: 103 read directly from the current OOH entry for the named SOC code, and 10 using the all-occupation projection of +3.5% where graduates disperse too widely for a single occupation to represent them.
NCES IPEDS and the CIP–SOC crosswalk
US federal database

National Center for Education Statistics. Enrolment, completions and program data by CIP code across all US institutions; the crosswalk is the published bridge from program to occupation. nces.ed.gov/ipeds/cipcode

Used for: Program identification and the CIP-to-SOC bridge.

04The five scoring dimensions

Each dimension is expressed on a 0–1 risk scale, where 0 is no risk and 1 is maximum risk, then combined into a weighted composite.

DimensionVariableDefault weightWhat it measures
Task automation potentialtask40%The share of the occupation’s O*NET tasks that current AI can perform, from Eloundou et al. β scores cross-checked against the Felten index. The strongest single predictor of near-term disruption, and weighted accordingly.
Curriculum half-lifehalfLife20%Estimated years until roughly 40% or more of the program’s target tasks are automated at scale — the point where a curriculum built to today’s standards is substantially misaligned with what employers need. Shorter means higher risk.
Faculty AI readinessfaculty15%The estimated share of faculty in the program area with working AI fluency: able to use these tools in instruction and to assess AI-assisted student work critically. This is the implementation constraint. A program that must change may be unable to change quickly.
Regional labour marketregion15%A multiplier comparing local employer demand with the national average. Above 1.0 means local demand exceeds national; below 1.0 means it lags, which brings recommended changes forward.
Employment outlookempOut10%BLS ten-year projected change for the primary SOC code. Captures whether the labour market is contracting independently of AI, which compounds risk when exposure is also high.

On the choice of weights

The 40/20/15/15/10 default puts task automation first, consistent with how the Eloundou and Felten research frames the question, and distributes the remainder across implementation capacity, market context and timing. These are defensible choices, not derived ones. Every weight is adjustable in the application, and four presets are provided so that any conclusion can be tested against a different set of priorities before it is acted on. A recommendation that survives only one weighting is not a finding.

05Normalisation functions

The five dimensions arrive in different units — fractions, years, percentages, multipliers — so each is mapped onto a 0–1 risk scale before combination. These are the functions the application actually runs, reproduced verbatim.

// Task automation — already 0–1 from the source research
task = p.exposure

// Curriculum half-life — years until ~40% of tasks are automated
hl = min(1, max(0, 1 - (p.halfLife - 0.5) / 14.5))
//   0.5 yr → 1.00 (maximum urgency)   15 yr → 0.00

// Faculty readiness — inverted; less ready means more risk
fac = 1 - (facultyReady(p) / 100)

// Regional labour market — inverted multiplier, clamped both ends
reg = min(1, max(0, 1 - (region(p) - 0.8) / 0.4))
//   ≤0.80× → 1.00        ≥1.20× → 0.00

// Employment outlook — projected decline becomes risk
emp = max(0, min(1, -p.empOut / 0.15))
//   ≤-15% → 1.00         ≥0% → 0.00

Two fixes carried into v2

The regional function is now clamped at both ends. Previously a multiplier above 1.2 produced a negative risk contribution, which silently subtracted from the composite rather than bottoming out at zero.

The faculty and regional functions now read the institution’s own uploaded figures where they exist. In v1 the regional term read the national default even when a user had supplied a local multiplier, so the regional column of an uploaded CSV changed the displayed number in the program panel but not the score behind the recommendation.

06The composite score

compositeScore(p) =
    task × w.task          // task automation potential
  + hl   × w.halfLife      // curriculum half-life
  + fac  × w.faculty       // faculty readiness gap
  + reg  × w.regional      // regional labour market
  + emp  × w.employment    // employment outlook

// defaults, which must sum to 1.0
w = { task: 0.40, halfLife: 0.20, faculty: 0.15, regional: 0.15, employment: 0.10 }
// range [0, 1]; higher is greater risk

The composite is not the same quantity as the AI exposure percentage on each card. Exposure is one of five inputs. The composite can sit above or below it depending on the other four, and the program panel says which and why whenever the two differ by more than five points.

Why a linear model

A weighted linear combination was chosen over multiplicative, fuzzy or non-linear aggregation for three reasons: an administrator can see directly how moving a weight moves the output; the calculation can be reproduced by hand from the numbers on screen; and the quality of the underlying data does not justify the precision a more elaborate model would imply. Sophistication in the aggregation would be borrowed authority.

07Classification thresholds

Rules are applied in order. The first that matches decides the recommendation.

#ConditionResultReasoning
1pipeline = narrow
AND mechanism = rep
AND empOut ≤ −0.03
AND composite > 0.62		● RestructureAll four at once: the credential trains for essentially one occupation, AI substitutes for rather than augments the core tasks, employment is genuinely projected to contract, and overall risk is high. Indicates a merge, a teach-out or a substantial repositioning with a defined pathway for enrolled students.
2composite > 0.555◆ PivotHigh risk, but graduates are still being hired. The curriculum must move from performing the tasks to directing and auditing the systems that perform them.
3composite < 0.36▲ ExpandLow risk. The work resists substitution — physical skill, direct care, accountable judgement — and demand is stable or growing. The constraint is capacity.
4otherwise■ AugmentModerate risk. Add AI fluency without redesign; review annually.

Why rule 1 was rewritten

Version 1 required only mechanism = rep AND composite > 0.67. Every program flagged as substitution-type cleared 0.67, so in practice the flag alone decided the outcome, and the flag was being set from task content. The result: English, creative writing, journalism, photography, foreign languages, paralegal studies and medical assisting were all recommended for wind-down.

Checked against BLS, medical assistants are projected to grow 13%, medical records specialists 8%, paralegals and interpreters roughly flat, writers flat, court reporters flat. Only journalism showed the decline v1 assumed. The tool was reading “AI can do this task” as “this occupation is disappearing,” which does not follow, and was most damaging exactly where a public university can least afford an unfounded claim.

With the pipeline and employment tests added, 2 of 113 programs now meet rule 1: Insurance & Risk Management, whose target occupation (claims adjusters) BLS projects at −5%, and Network Administration, whose target occupation (network and computer systems administrators) BLS projects at −4%. Both are single-occupation credentials in fields where AI substitutes for the core task. That is a substantive claim about the labour market, not a softening: only 7 of 113 programs feed an occupation projected to shrink at all.

Current distribution at default weights: 41 Pivot, 32 Expand, 38 Augment, 2 Restructure.

Threshold sensitivity

The 0.555 and 0.36 cut points are calibrated choices, not natural kinds. A program scoring 0.550 is not meaningfully different from one scoring 0.560, yet they appear in different categories. 28 of 113 programs sit within 0.04 of a boundary, and the application marks each of them Borderline on the card rather than leaving the reader to discover it. Programs whose category changes across the four weighting presets are additionally marked Sensitive to weighting.

08Task-level scoring

Each program panel shows four to six representative tasks for the target occupation, each with an impact score and a classification. Task statements come from O*NET for the mapped SOC code. The score estimates how much of that task current AI can perform at acceptable quality — an estimate of capability, not an observed displacement rate.

Task characterClassificationBasis
Pattern recognition, text and code generation, data processing, translation, scheduling, routingAI replaces
score 0.65–0.98		Well within demonstrated capability of current frontier models. Calibrated to Eloundou β for the task category.
Analysis, research, synthesis, recommendation subject to human reviewAI assists
score 0.40–0.75		AI substantially accelerates the work while human judgement remains necessary for quality. The score reflects how much of the effort shifts.
Physical manipulation, direct patient or client interaction, novel judgement, real-time situational responseAI assists
score 0.02–0.25		Dependent on embodied action, social presence or contextual judgement that current systems cannot replicate.

The program-level mechanism flag is set from the modal task — the highest-importance, highest-frequency one. A program flagged rep has its central recurring work targeted for substitution rather than acceleration. Since v2 that flag informs the recommendation but, on its own, no longer determines it.

09The “urgent and under-resourced” filter

isCritical(p) =
      p.exposure ≥ 0.65        // high task exposure
  AND p.halfLife ≤ 3.0         // under three years of runway
  AND facultyReady(p) ≤ 35     // faculty not positioned to respond
  AND verdict(p) ≠ "expand"    // added in v2

This combination is worth isolating because it identifies programs where the problem is pressing and the institution’s capacity to respond is constrained at the same time. A program needing curriculum overhaul inside three years, with fewer than a third of its faculty holding relevant fluency, cannot move at the pace the situation asks without deliberate investment beginning now.

The thresholds mark operational significance rather than statistical significance: 65% exposure is a clear majority of tasks affected; three years is the outer edge of an ordinary annual review cycle; 35% readiness is roughly where a critical mass for change cannot be assembled without a structured development program. The exclusion of Expand programs was added in v2 because a growing, low-substitution program with unprepared faculty has a staffing problem, not an urgency problem, and mixing the two obscured both.

10Worked example: Accounting

Accounting, CIP 52.0301, mapped to SOC 13-2011 (Accountants and auditors), at default weights.

Inputs. exposure = 0.83; halfLife = 1.8 years; facultyReady = 22%; region = 0.90×; empOut = 5% BLS verified (SOC 13-2011, 2025-35); mechanism = aug; pipeline = broad.

Rule 1 requires a narrow pipeline; Accounting is broad, so it does not apply regardless of the other conditions. The composite of 0.744 exceeds 0.555, so the result is ◆ Pivot.

Reading the two numbers together

The composite (0.744) is lower than raw task exposure (0.83). Accounting is among the most task-exposed programs in the set, but accountants and auditors are projected to grow 5% over the decade, so the employment dimension contributes nothing to risk and pulls the composite down. That gap is the whole argument: the work is changing sharply while the demand for people who can do it is not falling. Version 1 of this document asserted the opposite direction here, which was an error.

11Every employment figure and where it comes from

Every program in Meridian carries a projected ten-year employment change, and that figure feeds the composite score directly. Version 1 described these as drawn from BLS. On checking each one against the Occupational Outlook Handbook, 96 of 113 did not match — some by margins large enough to reverse the recommendation.

All 113 are now documented. 103 were read directly from the OOH entry for the program's SOC code in the current 2025–35 round. 10 are degrees whose graduates disperse too widely for any single occupational projection to represent them; those use the BLS all-occupation projection of +3.5% rather than an asserted figure. None remains an unsupported estimate.

The four that mattered most

Medical assistants were recorded at −4%. BLS projects +13%, among the faster-growing occupations in the country. The program had been recommended for wind-down.

Medical records specialists, the occupation behind Medical Billing & Coding, were recorded at −12%. BLS projects +8%. This program was the worked example of a dying field in the v1 documentation.

Court reporters and simultaneous captioners were recorded at −18%. BLS projects little or no change. (Its SOC code was also wrong: court reporters moved to 27-3092 under the 2018 SOC.)

Interpreters and translators, behind Foreign Languages, were recorded at −8%. BLS projects +2%.

These were not rounding differences. They were the load-bearing evidence for the most consequential recommendations the tool made.

Three cautions

Group pages versus SOC rows. Many OOH pages cover a group of occupations and publish a headline figure for the group. Where the SOC code sits inside such a group, the figure here is the SOC-specific row from the page's projections table, not the headline. Mathematicians (+1%) and statisticians (+11%) share a page whose headline is +10%; using the headline would have been badly wrong for one of them.

"Indicative" occupations. 20 programs are marked indicative: the named occupation is the largest single destination for graduates but still a minority of them. Psychology is mapped to clinical and counselling psychologists, English to writers and authors. The figure is real; its relevance to the whole cohort is partial, and the panel says so.

Projections are models. BLS employment projections are the best publicly available account of occupational demand, and they have historically been conservative about technological displacement. They are one considered view, not settled fact about 2035. The all-occupation figure for this round is +3.5%, down from 10.9% over 2015–25, so what reads as "growth" in this dataset is often growth relative to an unusually flat baseline.

13Calibration and disagreement

Every program carries a second field alongside the computed score: the author’s own assessment of what that program needs, recorded independently of the formula. The thresholds in section 7 were then chosen to maximise agreement between the two. At default weights the model reproduces the human assessment for 96 of 113 programs (85%).

In version 1 the human assessment was recorded in the data but never read. The displayed recommendation came entirely from the formula, which disagreed with it for 29 programs — so a quarter of the site said something its author had not concluded, with no indication that this was happening.

Version 2 keeps both and shows the disagreement. Where the model and the recorded assessment differ, the program is marked Contested and the panel names both verdicts. A disagreement between a formula and a considered human judgement is information, and burying it would be the wrong instinct for a tool whose purpose is to start a conversation.

14Limitations and appropriate use

These apply to every use of Meridian and should be passed on to any audience relying on its outputs.

⏱

A point-in-time snapshot with no automatic refresh. Employment figures were read from the 2025–35 OOH round in September 2026. BLS advances its projection window roughly annually, and each new round revises figures materially — physician assistants moved from +25% to +21% between the two most recent rounds, and graphic designers from +2% to −2%. Exposure research and O*NET task data move too, and AI capability fastest of the three. Re-read section 11 against the current OOH round at least every eighteen months; programs within ±0.05 of a threshold are the most vulnerable.

📍

National figures, not institutional ones. Faculty readiness, regional demand and enrolment vary enormously between institutions. The CSV overlay exists precisely for this; a score computed from national defaults describes no real institution.

🎓

A program category is not a program. Meridian scores “Accounting,” not your accounting department. Some are already deeply AI-integrated and some are not. These figures describe the modal program.

⚖️

Thresholds are choices. 0.555 and 0.36 are defensible, not derived. A program at 0.550 and one at 0.560 are not meaningfully different, yet they display differently. Use the weight sliders on any borderline program before acting.

🚫

This does not forecast AI. Scores reflect the current generation of systems. They make no attempt to anticipate capabilities two or three generations out, which the cited research does not support and which nobody can presently do well.

🏛️

Not a substitute for formal review. Decisions about restructuring, resource reallocation or teach-out belong to curriculum review, accreditation consultation, faculty governance and institution-specific labour market analysis. Meridian tells you where to start that work.

🔢

Whole percentages, group pages, and the baseline. OOH publishes whole percentages, so figures here carry no decimals. Where a SOC sits inside a broader OOH page, the SOC row is used rather than the page headline. Ten dispersed degrees use the all-occupation baseline of +3.5% instead of a single occupational figure — a deliberate refusal to assert what the evidence does not show, not a measurement.

🧭

Exposure is not desert. A high score says AI can perform tasks the program teaches. It says nothing about whether the discipline is worth studying, whether its graduates lead good lives, or what a university is for. Those questions are prior to this one and are not answered by any of these numbers.

✨

The optional AI features generate text, never numbers. Meridian can be connected to a language model of your choosing — including one running locally on your own machine — to draft a memo, committee questions or an advising note. That feature is off unless you switch it on, and it changes nothing about how any score is computed. The model is handed the figures this page has already calculated and is instructed to introduce no statistic it was not given. It is still a language model: every output is labelled as generated and unverified, carries the provider and model that produced it, and is advisory. Do not send student data to a cloud provider through it; the local options exist for exactly that reason. The key you supply is stored in your browser and sent only to the provider you chose, which also means the provider’s terms and data retention apply to whatever you send, under your own contract rather than your institution’s.

📊

Not a primary source. Cite Meridian as an analytical instrument. Attribute specific numerical claims to the underlying sources, noting that the program-level figure involves methodological judgement.
