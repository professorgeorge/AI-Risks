A practical guide

How people actually use Meridian

Ten real situations, in plain language. No technical background required — if you teach, study, advise, plan or hire, one of these is close to your question.

Who it is for

Six audiences, one dataset. The lens selector at the top of the dashboard rewrites the page for whichever one you are.

🎓

Students and applicants

Find out what the degree you are considering actually prepares you for, which parts of that work AI is taking over, and what to add so you graduate on the right side of the line.

📚

Faculty and department chairs

See a task-level breakdown of the occupations your program feeds, and which of your assignments have stopped measuring what you think they measure.

🏛️

Deans, provosts and planning offices

Understand which programs need attention, which are stable ground for investment, and what the whole portfolio looks like on one screen.

⚖️

Policy and workforce analysts

Program-level exposure mapped to SOC codes and federal projections, to see where public training capacity and labour demand diverge.

💼

Employers and workforce partners

What the next cohort was trained to do, how much of it is now automated, and which pipelines are tightening.

🧭

Advisers and careers services

Evidence to replace the headlines students arrive with, and a shareable link straight to any program.

Ten scenarios

Each one names the lens to select and the exact steps to follow. The numbers quoted are the numbers the site currently shows.

01
Student · choosing a major
Lens: Student or applicant

“Everyone tells me English is a dead end now that AI writes. Is that true?”

A second-year student is under pressure from family to switch out of English. She wants to know what the evidence says rather than what the headlines say.

  1. Choose the Student or applicant lens in the bar under the navigation. Everything on the page is rewritten for her question rather than a provost’s.
  2. Search English / Literature and open the card.
  3. Read the task breakdown. Drafting and editing score high for AI substitution. But the employment line reads 0% — flat, carrying a BLS verified tag: writers and authors are projected for little or no change, and most English graduates do not become writers by trade anyway.
  4. Read the verdict. It reads Pivot, not Restructure: the work is changing, the demand is not collapsing.
  5. Scroll to What to add to your plan for the specific additions worth making.

ResultShe leaves with a defensible answer rather than a mood. The honest version is that the writing tasks are heavily exposed and the occupational demand is flat — which is an argument for what she adds to the degree, not for abandoning it. The panel also tells her that writers and authors is the largest single destination for English graduates rather than where most of them go, which is the part her family is missing. Version 1 of this tool would have told her to get out, on an employment figure the federal data does not support.

02
Department chair, Nursing · curriculum committee
Lens: Faculty member

“Someone told the faculty that AI is coming for nursing. What is actually true?”

A nursing chair wants to know what the evidence supports before proposing anything to a sceptical faculty.

  1. Choose the Faculty member lens.
  2. Search Nursing and open the BSN card.
  3. Read the verdict: Expand, with task exposure of 31% and a runway of 5–8 years. Registered nurses are projected to grow 6%, BLS verified.
  4. In the task breakdown, documentation and scheduling carry meaningful AI assistance; physical assessment, clinical judgement and family communication score very low.
  5. Check the flags on the card. This program sits near a category boundary, so it is marked Borderline — worth saying aloud in the meeting rather than presenting the label as settled.
  6. Read the recommended moves. The leading item is a focused module on interpreting AI-flagged patient risk scores.

ResultThe chair goes to the faculty with a specific, evidence-grounded proposal — one new module, expanded simulation hours — and with the honest caveat that the program sits close to a line. That caveat buys more credibility than a confident label would.

03
Dean of Business · annual program review
Lens: Dean or provost

“Accounting, Finance and Marketing are all exposed. Do they need the same response?”

Three programs, three different underlying situations. The dean wants to see the difference concretely before allocating a limited faculty development budget.

  1. Click the Business field tile.
  2. Use + Compare on the Accounting, Finance and Marketing cards, then open the comparison.
  3. Read across the rows. Accounting: 83% exposure, 22% faculty readiness. Marketing: 76% exposure, 44% readiness. Similar exposure, very different capacity to respond.
  4. Check the employment row. All three show growth or stability, so none of this is an enrolment argument.

ResultThe budget goes to Accounting first — not because its AI risk is worse, but because the people who would have to teach a revised curriculum are furthest from ready. Marketing can move faster on its own. That is a different planning problem and it needs a different instrument.

04
Provost · board preparation
Lens: Dean or provost

“The board is asking about AI and the curriculum. I have twenty minutes.”

A defensible institutional summary is needed, drawn from public federal data rather than vendor claims, without a research project.

  1. Open the dashboard. No setup, no upload.
  2. Switch to Field overview to see all twelve fields with their response mix in one screen.
  3. Return to Programs and read the four counters: 41 Pivot, 32 Expand, 38 Augment, 2 Restructure.
  4. Click Print / PDF. The output carries the date, the filters in force, the weighting used and whether institutional data was applied — so the board can see the assumptions, not just the conclusion.

ResultThe headline is more useful than the alarm the board arrived with: across 113 programs, exactly 2 meets the bar for wind-down planning, while 41 need curriculum work. The institution’s problem is curricular capacity, not portfolio contraction. That reframing is worth the twenty minutes on its own.

05
Institutional researcher · vetting the numbers
Lens: Policy or workforce

“Before this goes to a committee, where does each number actually come from?”

An IR office has been asked to vet the tool. Their first question is the right one: which figures would survive a challenge.

  1. Open any program panel and look at the three headline figures. Each carries a tag: BLS verified, BLS baseline, or Estimate.
  2. Task exposure and curriculum runway are tagged Estimate throughout. They derive from published research but involve judgement, and the tool says so at the point of use rather than in a footnote.
  3. Employment outlook is tagged BLS verified for 103 of the 113 programs, read from the Occupational Outlook Handbook entry for the SOC code shown in the panel header. Ten dispersed degrees carry BLS baseline instead.
  4. Read the occupational mapping section at the foot of the panel. For broad degrees it states plainly that the named occupation is the largest single destination, not where most graduates go.
  5. Open methodology §11 for the complete record: every program, its SOC code, its figure and its evidence tier.

ResultThe committee gets a clear answer about evidentiary weight. Employment is sourced and checkable at bls.gov in a minute; exposure and runway are reasoned estimates; and the tool distinguishes between them everywhere rather than presenting one uniform surface of confidence.

06
Policy analyst · state workforce board
Lens: Policy or workforce

“Where is public training capacity pointed at occupations that are actually contracting?”

A state board is reviewing which programs receive workforce funding and wants an evidence base that will survive scrutiny.

  1. Choose the Policy or workforce lens. Cards now lead with the SOC code and the projected employment change.
  2. Open Field overview for aggregate exposure and response mix by field.
  3. Filter to Restructure to see where substitution and contraction genuinely coincide.
  4. Sort by Most exposed and read down the employment column, checking which figures carry the BLS verified tag and which are estimates.
  5. Open methodology §11 for the full list of employment figures that were corrected, with SOC codes and cycles.

ResultThe board finds far less contraction than expected: seven of 113 programs feed an occupation BLS projects will shrink at all, and two combine that with the task substitution and single-occupation dependence that would justify winding a program down. The defensible recommendation is retraining and faculty development in the high-churn pipelines, not capacity cuts.

07
Employer · regional hiring partner
Lens: Employer

“What will the graduates we hire next year actually be able to do?”

A regional employer hires from several local programs and is deciding where to put apprenticeship and placement effort.

  1. Choose the Employer lens. Cards now lead with the most-automated task in each program.
  2. Filter to Expand to see where graduate supply is tight and AI will not loosen it.
  3. Note the trades: HVAC Technology, Welding Technology, Automotive Technology and others show long runways and regional demand above the national average.
  4. Open a program you hire from and read the task breakdown to see what its graduates were trained to do.

ResultPlacement and apprenticeship effort moves to the programs where supply is genuinely constrained, and the conversation with those departments becomes specific: here is the task we still need people for, here is the one we no longer do by hand.

08
Planning analyst · institutional data overlay
Lens: Dean or provost

“National averages do not describe us. Can I see our actual picture?”

Enrolment is unusually high in Nursing and faculty AI readiness unusually low in Business. The analyst wants to know whether that changes anything.

  1. Click Your data in the navigation.
  2. Download the template. It contains all 113 program names already spelled correctly, so nothing has to be typed.
  3. Fill in only the rows you have figures for. Leave the rest blank; blanks fall back to the national default.
  4. Drop the file back in. Programs using your figures gain a ★ Your data badge and the scores recalculate immediately — including the regional multiplier, which in version 1 was displayed but silently ignored by the score.
  5. The file never leaves the browser. It is stored locally on that device and can be removed with one click.

ResultThe picture that emerges belongs to the institution rather than to a national average. A program that looks stable nationally can look urgent once real faculty readiness is plugged in, and the reverse happens just as often.

09
Department chair · turning data into a document
Lens: Faculty member

“I have the evidence. Now I have to write the paper nobody wants to write.”

A chair has forty minutes before a deadline and needs a first draft of a curriculum memo that will not embarrass her in front of a dean.

  1. Click AI assist in the navigation and read the disclosure. Choose a provider. If the question touches anything sensitive, choose Ollama or LM Studio, which run on your own machine so nothing is transmitted.
  2. Paste your key, or for a local model just follow the setup steps shown. Add a few lines of institutional context: size, mission, region, what your budget will actually bear.
  3. Open your program and scroll to AI assistance at the foot of the panel.
  4. Choose Draft a brief for my institution. The model receives the program’s figures and your context, and nothing else. It is instructed to introduce no statistic it was not given, and to flag anything the tool has marked uncertain.
  5. Read it properly, then Copy text — the copy carries a provenance line naming the model and the time, so the draft cannot quietly lose its label on the way into a document.
  6. Before sending it, run Argue against this verdict and read that too. If the counter-case is strong, your memo should say so.

ResultA first draft in under a minute, with its provenance attached and the counter-argument considered. What it is not is a finished document: it was written by a language model that knows only the figures on one page and the three lines you typed. Treat it as a colleague’s rough draft, which is genuinely useful and is not the same thing as being right.

10
Provost · strategic planning retreat
Lens: Dean or provost

“Does the answer change if we weight things differently?”

The cabinet is being asked to accept a set of priorities. The provost wants the group to test the conclusion rather than adopt it.

  1. Open Methodology and read the disclosure at the top, which states plainly what is sourced and what is estimated.
  2. Try the Faculty-readiness focus preset, which weights institutional capacity heavily, and apply it.
  3. Compare the resulting counters with the Balanced default. Programs that move between categories are the ones worth the discussion.
  4. Look for cards marked Sensitive to weighting — these change category across the presets and should not be decided on one run.

ResultThe meeting becomes a structured argument about assumptions rather than a dispute about whose numbers to trust. That changing the weights visibly changes the output is itself the point: the right response to AI depends on what your institution can actually do.

Every feature, in plain English

What each control does and why it exists.

🧭

The audience lens

Five settings — student, faculty, dean or provost, policy, employer. Each rewrites the verdict text, the card summary and the action list for that reader. The underlying data never changes; only the question being asked of it does.

📊

Portfolio counters

Four live counts above the grid. They follow the field filter, so selecting a college shows that college’s mix. Click any counter to filter to it.

🗺️

Field overview

Every field on one screen with mean exposure and a stacked bar of its response mix. The fastest way to brief someone who has two minutes.

🚨

Urgent and under-resourced

High exposure, short runway and low faculty readiness at once — a pressing problem in a program that cannot currently respond to it. One click instead of manual cross-referencing.

🏷️

Borderline and Contested flags

Borderline marks a program within 0.04 of a category boundary. Contested marks one where the model and the author’s own assessment disagree. Both appear on the card rather than being buried in the documentation.

✅

Evidence tags

Three tiers, shown wherever a number appears. BLS verified: read from the Occupational Outlook Handbook entry for that program’s SOC code. BLS baseline: a dispersed degree using the all-occupation projection of +3.5% rather than an asserted figure. Estimate: reasoned from published research.

✨

Optional AI assistance

Off by default and entirely optional. Connect a model of your choosing — Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, OpenRouter, Mistral, xAI, or Ollama and LM Studio running locally on your own machine — and Meridian will draft a memo, six committee questions, an advising note, a plain-language explanation, or the strongest case that its own verdict is wrong. It writes only from the figures on the page, never touches a score, and labels everything it produces as generated and unverified. There is no account and no server: your key stays in your browser and goes only to the provider you picked.

🖥️

Local models, no data leaving

Ollama and LM Studio run on your own computer, so nothing is transmitted anywhere. This is the option to use if the question involves anything you would not paste into a commercial service. Works in Chrome, Edge and Firefox; Safari blocks local connections from a web page and will say so plainly rather than failing silently.

🗂️

Occupational mapping

Each panel names the SOC code behind the figures, and says when that occupation is the largest single destination for graduates rather than where most of them go. For ten dispersed degrees it says plainly that no single occupation represents the cohort.

⏱

Runway as a band

Curriculum half-life is shown as “2–3 years” rather than “2.4 years”. The estimate is not precise to a decimal and the display no longer pretends otherwise.

⚖️

Side-by-side compare

Up to three programs across seven dimensions, including the employment row with its evidence tags.

🔗

Shareable links

Filters, sort, lens and the open program are all carried in the URL. Send a colleague exactly the view you are looking at.

⌨️

Keyboard and screen reader

Every control is reachable by keyboard. Press / to jump to search and Esc to close any panel. Risk categories carry a distinct glyph as well as a colour.

⚙️

Weight sliders and presets

Adjust the five dimensions or use a preset. Any conclusion that survives only one weighting is not a conclusion.

📤

Your own data

CSV overlay for enrolment, faculty readiness and regional demand. Stored locally in your browser; never uploaded anywhere.

🖨️

Print and PDF

Prints the current view, or the open program brief, with the date, active filters, weighting and overlay status on the page.

📐

Full methodology

Formulas, thresholds, a worked example, the complete list of corrected employment figures, and every disagreement between the model and its author.

Getting the most out of it

  • Set the lens before you read anything. The same program reads very differently to a student and to a provost, and the default is the provost’s view. If you are not one, change it.
  • Read the task breakdown before the verdict. The verdict is a summary; the tasks are the evidence. Disagreements about what a program should do usually turn out to be disagreements about which tasks matter.
  • Check the evidence tag on any number you plan to quote. Employment figures are sourced and checkable at bls.gov in a minute. Task exposure and curriculum runway are reasoned estimates and should be described that way.
  • Treat Borderline and Contested as invitations, not warnings. Those are the programs where the tool is least confident and a conversation is most valuable.
  • Change the weights before you conclude anything. A recommendation that only appears under one weighting is not a recommendation.
  • Do not use it to close a program on its own. Of 113 programs, 2 meet the bar for wind-down planning, and even those are prompts to check your own placement data rather than decisions.
  • If you switch on AI assistance, read what it writes before anyone else does. It drafts from the figures on the page and is told to invent nothing, but it is a language model and it does not know your institution. Every output is labelled generated and unverified for a reason.
  • Overlay your own figures if you have them. National averages describe no actual institution. Faculty readiness and regional demand are the two most worth replacing.
