How the Scores Are Built — & What They Cannot Tell You

The framework behind every score in Meridian is grounded in peer-reviewed research and federal public data. The five sources listed below are all real, publicly available, and widely cited in the academic literature on AI and labor markets.

What is directly sourced: The occupation-level AI exposure research by Eloundou et al. (2023) and the Felten, Raj & Seamans Princeton AI Exposure Index both published SOC-level scores in their public appendices. The O*NET task frameworks, BLS employment projections, and NCES IPEDS program data are all downloadable from federal databases. The CIP-SOC crosswalk is an NCES-published bridge table.

What is estimated, not sourced: The specific numerical scores shown for each program — the exposure percentages, half-life years, and faculty readiness figures — are informed estimates derived from those sources, not direct database pulls. Translating a national occupational exposure score into a program-level score requires methodological judgment. Scores should be treated as directional indicators, not precise measurements.

What this tool is designed for: Strategic conversation and planning. It is well-suited for helping academic leaders prioritize which programs to examine more closely, structure a curriculum review, or frame a faculty development agenda. It is not a substitute for a formal institutional review with audited data.

What this tool cannot do: It cannot predict the future of AI development. It cannot account for institution-specific strengths, regional employer relationships, accreditation nuances, or political and budgetary realities. All recommendations should be reviewed in light of your institution's specific context.

A note on data currency: The scores in this version of Meridian reflect a fixed snapshot of the research and federal datasets available at the time of publication. The underlying landscape — AI capabilities, occupational exposure scores, BLS employment projections, and O*NET task data — changes continuously. This application does not automatically refresh or pull live data. Findings should be interpreted with that constraint in mind: what is true today about a program's exposure may shift meaningfully within 12 to 18 months, particularly for programs already close to a category boundary. Treat all scores as current estimates requiring periodic manual review, not as permanent classifications.

Primary Research Sources
📄 Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock (2023) — "GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models." OpenAI / arXiv:2303.10130. Occupation-level GPT-4 exposure scores (α and β) by SOC code.
📄 Felten, Raj & Seamans (2023) — "How Will Language Models Use Tools?" Princeton AI Exposure Index. Multi-round occupation-level scores available at aiindex.princeton.edu.
🏛️ O*NET Resource Center — U.S. Department of Labor. Task statements, importance and frequency ratings for 1,000+ occupations. onetcenter.org/db_releases.html
🏛️ BLS Occupational Employment & Wage Statistics (OEWS) — U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Employment projections by SOC and metro area. bls.gov/oes
🏛️ NCES IPEDS & CIP-SOC Crosswalk — National Center for Education Statistics. Program-to-occupation mapping using 6-digit CIP codes. nces.ed.gov/ipeds
